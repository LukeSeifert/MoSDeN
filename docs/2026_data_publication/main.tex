\begin{abstract}
This paper presents an analysis on the sensitivity of \gls{DNP} group parameters, half-lives $\tau_k$ and yields $\nu_{d,k}$, to uncertainties in their nuclear data using thermal fission of $^{235}$U for various datasets: the \gls{IAEA} beta-delayed neutron emission database, ENDF/B-VII.1, ENDF/B-VIII.0, JEFF-3.1.1, and JENDL-5.
These uncertainty-weighted sensitivities provide a detailed view of which \glspl{DNP} need more accurate data such that analyses using individual \glspl{DNP}, such as microscopic group parameter formulation and summation calculations, may be more accurate.
Improving the accuracy of these analyses is of particular importance for kinetics models, which rely on accurate \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The microscopic approach for group parameter calculation is useful for providing insight into how each \gls{DNP} contributes to delayed neutron emission, but it requires accurate nuclear data. 
The analyses conducted in this work reveal several \glspl{DNP} that correlate strongly with the \gls{DNP} group parameters, indicating a need for more accurate data for those \glspl{DNP}.
There are also \glspl{DNP} which have large uncertainties and vary significantly between datasets which also warrant additional attention.
Large discrepancies are shown in ENDF datasets for $^{85}$As, $^{86}$As, $^{100}$Rb, and $^{137}$Sb; under prediction of the delayed neutron yield $\nu_d$ in JEFF-3.1.1 correlates to many small discrepancies in various \glspl{DNP}; and JENDL-5 is shown to offer good agreement with experimental and calculated delayed neutron yields.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modeling and simulation of nuclear reactors enables more accurate predictions about the reactor behavior during transient events, such as removal of a control rod.
To accurately model these dynamics, several different effects must be included: the change in the neutron density, the various feedback effects that influence the reactivity, the emission of prompt neutrons, and the emission of delayed neutrons.
These effects are well understood and have been simplified using various approximations.
For example, the delayed neutrons are treated as having six to eight sources, or \gls{DNP} groups, even though in reality there are hundreds of precursor nuclides that decay and emit these delayed neutrons \cite{brady1988evaluation}.

There are several different methods for calculating these \gls{DNP} groups, and in this work the microscopic approach is of particular interest \cite{d2002review, rudstam2002delayed, wilson2002delayed, brady1989delayed, mills2014calculation, huynh2014calculation, foligno_new_2019, piksaikin_new_2019}.
This approach uses experimental data for individual \glspl{DNP} combined together computationally.
One strength of this approach is that it provides insight into how each individual \gls{DNP} contributes to the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
However, this strength is also a weakness, as it relies on the nuclear data for every \gls{DNP} individually, which means a few large uncertainties in the various \glspl{DNP} can lead to uncertain results for the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
The microscopic approach can also be used for calculation of summed parameters such as the total delayed neutron yield $\nu_d$ and the average half-life $\bar\tau$ directly from the \gls{DNP} data, generally referred to as conducting a summation, or aggregate, calculation.

Investigations have been conducted in the literature using the microscopic approach to evaluate sensitivities and compare different datasets \cite{foligno_summation_2018, foligno_new_2019, mills2014calculation}.
Although these analyses are informative and show many of the differences between datasets, they do not discuss the importance of \glspl{DNP} in regards to their effect on the calculated \gls{DNP} group parameters but instead based on their contribution to the total delayed neutron yield.
Generally the larger the delayed neutron yield of a \gls{DNP}, the more important it is, but these analyses do not investigate how the uncertainty in each \gls{DNP} propagates through to calculated \gls{DNP} group parameters.

This work seeks to expand upon these previous analyses by using the microscopic approach in combination with Monte Carlo stochastic sampling of nuclear data uncertainties to evaluate uncertainty-weighted sensitivities of individual \glspl{DNP} to \gls{DNP} group half-lives and delayed neutron yields.
These results indicate which \glspl{DNP} in different datasets have large impacts on the \gls{DNP} group parameters, as well as which \glspl{DNP} have both large impacts and large uncertainties relative to those impacts.
By providing the data of importance for these datasets, future analyses can target experiments to produce those specific data to further improve \gls{DNP} group parameters and kinetics models.


\section{Methods}

This section details the three stages to the analyses presented in this paper: the data treated as an input, the solver which calculates the data of interest, and the data analysis which details how the solver results are processed.
The solver itself can be considered a converter of the \gls{DNP} nuclear data into six group \gls{DNP} parameters, which are then passed into data analysis calculations.

\subsection{DNP Nuclear Data Treatment}

There are two different sets of data used in this work: the nuclear data for each individual \gls{DNP} and the calculated \gls{DNP} group parameters that approximate those individual \glspl{DNP}.
The $I$ \glspl{DNP} have the following data:
\begin{align}
    I &= \text{the total number of \gls{DNP} [-]} \nonumber \\
    CFY_i &= \text{the cumulative fission yield of \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    P_{n,i} &= \text{the emission probability of \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    \tau_i &= \text{the half-life of \gls{DNP} $i$ $[s]$} \nonumber \\
    \chi_{d,i} &= \text{the delayed neutron energy spectrum from \gls{DNP} $i$ [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}
The cumulative fission yield represents the total production of that nuclide for a single fission, including decays that eventually lead to that nuclide.
The emission probability is the probability for a delayed neutron to be emitted from a given \gls{DNP}\footnote{This can alternatively be considered the number of delayed neutrons emitted per radioactive decay.}.
For this work, the delayed neutron energy spectra are not investigated, as it is considered out of scope.

The \gls{DNP} group parameters have the following data:
\begin{align}
    K &= \text{the total number of \gls{DNP} groups [-]} \nonumber \\
    \nu_{d,k} &= \text{the delayed neutron yield of group $k$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    \tau_k &= \text{the half-life of group $k$ $[s]$} \nonumber \\
    \chi_{d,k} &= \text{the delayed neutron energy spectrum from group $k$ [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}
The $K$ groups are organized such that the longest-lived group is number one, while the shortest-lived group is \gls{DNP} group $K$.
The delayed neutron yield for each group $\nu_{d,k}$ combines the emission probabilities and cumulative fission yields, as shown in Equation \eqref{eq:summation-tdny}, representing the number of delayed neutrons emitted from group $k$ from a single fission.

The datasets used in this work are given in Table \ref{tab:datasources}, including two different \gls{ENDF} datasets, the beta-delayed neutron emission database from the \gls{IAEA}, and \gls{JENDL}-5.
The \gls{IAEA} beta-delayed neutron emission database does not have cumulative fission yield data combined with it, though analyses using the data use \gls{JEFF}-3.1.1 cumulative fission yields \cite{DIMITRIOU2021144}.
For the analyses in this work, the cumulative fissions yields from all of the datasets will be used and compared against to offer a comprehensive analysis.

\subsection{Microscopic and Summation Calculations}

Within the microscopic approach, there are two distinct processes that can be used.
The summation calculation is the simpler of the two, and simply involves summing the nuclear data to calculate the total delayed neutron yield and average half-life.
The full microscopic approach is used to calculate the \gls{DNP} group parameters, which give time-dependency and can be used to calculate the total delayed neutron yield and average half-life from the \gls{DNP} group parameters as well as the \gls{DNP} nuclear data.

Equations \eqref{eq:summation-tdny} and \eqref{eq:avg-hl} are the equations for the total delayed neutron yield and average half-life, respectively, from the summation calculation as:
\begin{equation}
    \nu_d = \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n,i} CFY_i = \sum_{i=1}^I \nu_{d,i},
    \label{eq:summation-tdny}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\tau} = \frac{1}{\nu_d} \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n,i} CFY_i  \tau_i.
    \label{eq:avg-hl}
\end{equation}

The uncertainties for each calculation are propagated as shown in Equations \eqref{eq:summation-tdny-delta} and \eqref{eq:avg-hl-delta} as: 
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\nu_d}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^I (\sigma_{P_{n,i}} CFY_i)^2 + (P_{n,i} \sigma_{CFY_i})^2,
    \label{eq:summation-tdny-delta}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{align}
    \sigma_{\bar{\tau}}^2 = \frac{1}{\nu_d^2} \sum_{i=1}^I& (\sigma_{P_{n,i}} CFY_i \tau_i)^2 + (P_{n,i} \sigma_{CFY_i}  \tau_i)^2 \nonumber \\ 
    &+ (P_{n,i} CFY_i \sigma_{\tau_i})^2 + \frac{1}{\nu_d^2} ( P_{n,i} CFY_i \tau_i \sigma_{\nu_d})^2.
    \label{eq:avg-hl-delta}
\end{align}
These calculations are automated in the solver using the uncertainties Python package \cite{uncertaintiespackage}.
The standard deviation propagated in the summation calculation are given by the dataset used, and any values without a standard deviation or a standard deviation of 0 use a value of $10^{-12}$.

The microscopic approach enables calculation of time-dependent parameters by calculating the delayed neutron count rate from each \gls{DNP} after irradiation of a fissile nuclide.
The solver first calculates the concentration of each \gls{DNP}, followed by the delayed neutron count rate, and finally uses the SciPy optimize module to calculate the six \gls{DNP} group parameters that optimally fit that delayed neutron count rate \cite{scipy}.
To calculate sensitivities and propagate uncertainties, a Monte Carlo sampling technique is used \cite{foligno_new_2019}.
The solution is calculated with tolerances of $10^{-11}$ for ten randomly sampled initial conditions for the nominal nuclear data, which is then passed as an initial guess for each of the sampled Monte Carlo solves.
The concentrations have the uncertainty propagated using the uncertainties package, and then in the count rate calculation the concentration $N_i$, half-life $\tau_i$, and emission probability $P_{n,i}$ are sampled using a normal distribution with a different random value for each \gls{DNP}.

The concentration of each \gls{DNP} is calculated assuming the sample has reached equilibrium\footnote{The parasitic absorption and transmutation cross-sections of the \glspl{DNP} are negligible and are not included in this equation.} \cite{huynh2014calculation}:
\begin{equation}
    N_{i}(t=\infty) = \frac{CFY_i}{\lambda_i},
    \label{eq:conc}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    \lambda_i &= \text{the decay constant for \gls{DNP} $i$ $[s^{-1}]$.} \nonumber 
\end{align}

The delayed neutron count rate can then be calculated as shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:count-rate} as:
\begin{align}
    \dot{n}_d(t) &= \sum_{i=1}^I P_{n,i} N_i(t=\infty) e^{-\lambda_i t} \nonumber\\ 
    &= \sum_{i=1}^I \nu_{d,i} \lambda_{i}^{-1} e^{-\lambda_i t}.
    \label{eq:count-rate}
\end{align}
The count rate is first calculated using the nominal nuclear data before this calculation is repeated using the independently, normally sampled nuclear data for each \gls{DNP}.

Once the count rate is calculated, a non-linear least squares calculation can be used to determine the combination of \gls{DNP} group parameters that most optimally fits the calculated delayed neutron count rate.
The coefficient matrix and solution vector for the non-linear least squares solve are based on the \gls{DNP} group parameter fit assuming a constant fission rate of one fission per second for the delayed neutron count rate, given in Equation~\eqref{eq:count-rate-group} as \cite{wilson2002delayed}:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{n}_d(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \nu_{d,k} e^{-\lambda_i t}.
    \label{eq:count-rate-group}
\end{equation}
Once the delayed neutron count rate is acquired, an over-determined non-linear least squares approach is used to calculate the group yields and decays \cite{brady1988evaluation, chandler1969stepit, waldo1981delayed}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:lstsq} gives the general form of the least squares equation as:
\begin{equation}
A \vec{x} \approx \vec{b},
\label{eq:lstsq}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    A &= \text{the coefficient matrix [-]} \nonumber \\
    x &= \text{the solution vector $[\# \cdot s^{-1}]$} \nonumber \\
    b &= \text{the target vector $[\# \cdot s^{-1}]$.} \nonumber
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda} gives the coefficient matrix as:
\begin{equation}
A = \begin{bmatrix}
e^{-\lambda_1 t_0} & e^{-\lambda_2 t_0} & \hdots &  e^{-\lambda_K t_0}\\
e^{-\lambda_1 t_1} & e^{-\lambda_2 t_1} & \hdots &  e^{-\lambda_K t_1}\\
\vdots                       & \vdots                       & \ddots &  \vdots                      \\
e^{-\lambda_1 t_m} & e^{-\lambda_2 t_m} &  \hdots  & e^{-\lambda_K t_m}\\
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
The solution vector contains only the group yields, given in Equation~\eqref{eq:lstsq-solvec} as:
\begin{equation}
\vec{x} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
{\nu}_{d, 1}\\
{\nu}_{d, 2}\\
\vdots\\
{\nu}_{d, K}\\
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:lstsq-solvec}
\end{equation}
The target vector contains the delayed neutron count rate data for $m$ time-steps, shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:lstsq-target} as:
\begin{equation}
\vec{b} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
 \dot{n}_d(t_0)  \\
 \dot{n}_d(t_1) \\
\vdots\\
\dot{n}_d(t_m)\\
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:lstsq-target}
\end{equation}

Using least squares with a non-relative residual would lead to early time-step count rates dominating due to their larger magnitudes.
This is resolved by minimizing a relative residual, given by Equation~\eqref{eq:residual} as:
\begin{equation}
    r = \min_x \left| \left| \frac{\vec{b} - A\vec{x}}{\vec{b}} \right| \right|_2,
    \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
where the Euclidean norm, $||\cdot||_2$, is defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:euc-norm} as:
\begin{equation}
    ||\vec{x}||_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2}.
    \label{eq:euc-norm}
\end{equation}
The relative residual is used instead of a chi-squared statistic because the Monte Carlo sampling method is already in place to calculate and propagate the uncertainties \cite{Bevington2003Data}.

The \gls{DNP} group parameters can then be used to evaluate the total delayed neutron yield and average half-life, similar to Equations \eqref{eq:summation-tdny} and \eqref{eq:avg-hl}.
This is shown in Equations \eqref{eq:summation-tdny-group} and \eqref{eq:avg-hl-group} as:
\begin{equation}
    \nu_d \approx \sum_{k=1}^K \nu_{d,k},
    \label{eq:summation-tdny-group}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\tau} \approx \frac{1}{\nu_d} \sum_{k=1}^K \nu_{d,k} \tau_k.
    \label{eq:avg-hl-group}
\end{equation}
These equations are approximate because the number of groups $K$ used is fewer than the total number of \glspl{DNP} $I$.
To differentiate between the calculation of these parameters using $I$ \glspl{DNP} and $K$ groups, the parameters will be written as $\nu_d(I)$ and $\bar{\tau}(I)$ when calculated using $I$ \glspl{DNP} and $\nu_d(K)$ and $\bar{\tau}(K)$ when calculated using $K$ groups.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analysis}

The \gls{PCC} is used to analyze the \gls{DNP} group parameters calculated from the solver for various unique combinations of sampled \gls{DNP} nuclear data and is calculated using the SciPy stats module \cite{scipy}.
The \gls{PCC} measures the linear correlation between the \gls{DNP} data and the calculated \gls{DNP} group parameters.
A measure of 0 indicates there is no correlation, a measure of +1 indicates perfect positive linear correlation, and a measure of $-1$ indicates perfect negative linear correlation.
For example, $^{87}$Br can be expected to have a \gls{PCC} near +1 for the impact of its half-life $\tau_i$ on the half-life of group one\footnote{Group one is the longest-lived \gls{DNP} group.} $\tau_1$.
This is because $^{87}$Br is the longest-lived \gls{DNP}\footnote{There are other \glspl{DNP} with longer half-lives, such as $^{211}$Tl, $^{210}$Tl, and $^{210}$Hg, but they live only tens of seconds longer and generally have negligible concentrations.}, so the longest-lived \gls{DNP} group will be highly dependent on its nuclear data.

The \gls{PCC} was selected to measure variation in the group parameters because the sensitivities did not show strong non-linear behavior.
The \glspl{PCC} are calculated for each \gls{DNP} group half-life $\tau_k$ and delayed neutron yield $\nu_{d,k}$ based on changes in each \glspl{DNP}' concentration $N_i$, half-life $\tau_i$, and emission probability $P_{n,i}$.
The \gls{PCC} for each specific combination can be represented as $PCC_{i,v,k}$, where $i$ represents the \gls{DNP} $i$; $v$ represents the \gls{DNP} value, such as the half-life, emission probability, or concentration; and $k$ represents the \gls{DNP} group.

To improve the analysis in this work, the $PCC_{i,v,k}$ data was combined to form more concise coefficients.
The absolute value is used because the strength of the correlations are the main consideration under investigation in this study.
The total \gls{PCC} for a specific \gls{DNP} $i$ is given in Equation~\eqref{eq:pcc-sum}:
\begin{equation}
    PCC_{i} = \sum_v \sum_k \left|PCC_{i,v,k}\right|,
    \label{eq:pcc-sum}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    v &= \text{the \gls{DNP} value, representing the half-life, emission probability,} \nonumber \\
    &\text{\;\;\;\;\;or concentration of \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    PCC_{i, v, k} &= \text{the \gls{PCC} for \gls{DNP} $i$ and value $v$ with \gls{DNP} group parameter $k$ [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}

These \glspl{PCC} can be combined with the relative uncertainty associated with each \gls{DNP} to capture the nuclides which both impact the \gls{DNP} group parameters most significantly and have large uncertainties.
Specifically, this is calculated in Equation~\eqref{eq:Uiv} as the relative uncertainties times the magnitude of the \gls{PCC} for a \gls{DNP} $i$ and each \gls{DNP} value $v$ as $U_{i}$:
\begin{equation}
    U_{i} = \sum_v \frac{\sigma_{x_{i,v}}}{x_{i,v}} \sum_k\left|PCC_{i,v,k}\right|,
    \label{eq:Uiv}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    U_{i} &= \text{ the total uncertainty-weighted sensitivity from the contribution of every \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    \sigma_{x_{i,v}} &= \text{the uncertainty in the \gls{DNP} value $v$ for \gls{DNP} $i$ [-]} \nonumber \\
    x_{i,v} &= \text{the \gls{DNP} value $v$, representing the half-life, emission probability,} \nonumber \\
    &\text{\;\;\;\;\;or concentration of \gls{DNP} $i$ [-].} \nonumber
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:Uiv} shows that the relative uncertainty for each nuclear data component that is randomly sampled, such as the emission probability, is multiplied by the summed magnitudes of the \gls{PCC} for each \gls{DNP} group parameter using that data.
These values are then all summed, giving the relative uncertainty-weighted \gls{PCC} for each \gls{DNP}, which indicates how much attention that specific \gls{DNP} needs.
This metric is larger when:
\begin{itemize}
    \item changes in the \gls{DNP}'s nuclear data cause large changes in the \gls{DNP} group parameter(s)
    \item the relative uncertainty in the \gls{DNP}'s nuclear data is large.
\end{itemize}

This can also be represented for any individual \gls{DNP} value by removing the summation over $v$.
This is shown in Equation~\eqref{eq:Uiv-no-sum} which gives the uncertainty-weighted \gls{PCC} for a given \gls{DNP} $i$ and nuclear data $v$ as:
\begin{equation}
    U_{i,v} = \frac{\sigma_{i,v}}{x_{i,v}} \sum_k\left|PCC_{i,v,k}\right|.
    \label{eq:Uiv-no-sum}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}
This section presents the results for different datasets of cumulative fission yields, emission probabilities, and half-lives.
Six \gls{DNP} groups are used for this analysis, as this number of groups is commonly used in the literature and generally captures the effects of the \glspl{DNP} fairly well \cite{brady1989delayed}.
Each sub-section will contain the following analyses for each dataset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the total delayed neutron yield calculated from summation of \glspl{DNP} $\nu_d(I)$ and from the group parameters $\nu_d(K)$
    \item the average half-life calculated from summation of \glspl{DNP} $\bar{\tau}(I)$ and from the group parameters $\bar{\tau}(K)$
    \item the six \glspl{DNP} with the largest yield contributions
    \item the tabulated ten largest \glspl{PCC} and uncertainty-weighted \glspl{PCC}
    \item a chart of nuclides made using \gls{ARMI} with summed \glspl{PCC} and uncertainty-weighted \glspl{PCC} \cite{armi}
    \item a bar chart with the most significant uncertainty-weighted \glspl{PCC}.
\end{itemize}

As shown in Table \ref{tab:datasources}, the \gls{IAEA} database does not include cumulative fission yield data.
For that analysis, the JENDL-5 cumulative fission yields are used since they are the most recent data.
However, part of the analysis will also investigate the effects of hybrid datasets that vary the emission probability, half-life, and cumulative fission yield data.

This work is also verified with total delayed neutron yields from a related \gls{IAEA} \gls{CRP} analysis \cite{DIMITRIOU2021144}.
The analysis presented a benchmark problem using JEFF-3.1.1 cumulative fission yields with ENDF/B-VIII.0 emission probabilities and half-lives, which is replicated in this work \cite{santamarina2009jeff, Brown20181}.
The four groups in the study are \gls{CEA}, \gls{CIEMAT}, the \gls{JAEA}, and the University of Nantes, all of whom reported the same result of $0.01609 \pm 0.0008$ for the total delayed neutron yield from thermal fission of $^{235}$U.
This agrees with the same result calculated in this work of $0.01609 \pm 0.0008$.
This work is also verified with the total delayed neutron yield $\nu_d$ from Leconte et al. from JEFF-3.1.1 cumulative fission yields combined with ENDF/B-VII.1 emission probabilities and half-lives.
Specifically, this work calculates a value of $0.0157 \pm 0.0008$, which exactly agrees with the results from Leconte et al. \cite{leconte2020new}.
Finally, there is also agreement with Huynh et al. using the JEFF-3.1.1 data to calculate the total delayed neutron yield of $0.0148 \pm 0.0008$ \cite{huynh2014calculation}.
The verification uses the results from summation calculations, but each section demonstrates that the summation calculation results offer agreement within uncertainty bounds with the \gls{DNP} group parameter evaluated values.

For the first set of results, additional information will be included.
Specifically, the delayed neutron count rate will be provided as well as a comparison to the count rate calculated from other \gls{DNP} group parameters in the literature.
There will also be an example figure demonstrating how the stochastic sampling affects the \gls{DNP} group parameters.
Additionally, specific \gls{DNP} examples will be provided demonstrating the \glspl{PCC}.

\section{Conclusions}

This work presented analyses using the microscopic approach to compare \gls{DNP} nuclear data between the IAEA beta-delayed neutron emission database, ENDF/B-VII.1, ENDF/B-VIII.0, JEFF-3.1.1, JENDL-5, and hybrid datasets.
These results, when compared with other summation calculations in the literature and experimental data, indicate that each dataset has specific \glspl{DNP} that have large relative uncertainties with a greater impact on the calculation of \gls{DNP} group parameters and delayed neutron emission rates than other \glspl{DNP}.

Large differences in ENDF datasets that lead to discrepancies when comparing results with other summation calculations and experimental data are presented, specifically indicating problematic nuclides of $^{85}$As, $^{86}$As, $^{100}$Rb, $^{137}$Sb.
The under-prediction of JEFF-3.1.1 for calculation of the total delayed neutron yield is also presented, indicating that the largest individual differences come from $^{94}$Rb, $^{98m}$Y, $^{85}$As, and $^{89}$Br; however, the majority of the difference comes from small contributions from many \glspl{DNP} both present in the dataset and lack of contribution from \glspl{DNP} missing from the dataset.
The JENDL-5 dataset is shown to have good agreement with experiments and other calculations in the literature, including when using the IAEA beta-delayed neutron emission database.
The \glspl{DNP} with the largest uncertainty-weighted sensitivities that affect delayed neutron emission rates and calculated \gls{DNP} group parameters are provided for each dataset evaluated in this work.
A discussion on hybrid datasets is also provided, which provides evidence that cumulative fission yields in ENDF datasets contribute to larger delayed neutron yields than other results in the literature.

The results in this work offer a new perspective for identifying \glspl{DNP} in nuclear datasets that are in need of reduced uncertainties or lead to discrepancies with other results in the literature.
These results can be leveraged to resolve nuclear data discrepancies and conduct experiments to improve the most relevant \gls{DNP} nuclear data.
Through these targeted improvements to the nuclear data, future calculations using the individual \gls{DNP} nuclear data can be improved more rapidly.
These data will improve the accuracy of transient simulations for nuclear reactors and enable more in-depth analyses on \gls{DNP} behavior.
